Case Study

Performance Management Team Excellence Engine

Define “good” once, per role, with evidence, and performance management becomes a team sport.

  • Learn

  • People
  • Strategy

Opportunity

  • Performance conversations ran on impressions. Reviews felt subjective, and nobody shared a definition of “good.” Growth across the team was uneven, and development plans weren’t tied to evidence. The annual review didn’t help: it was high-level lip service, and the process wasn’t built to enforce rigor or accountability.
  • There was also a gap on the business side. The business could say which capabilities it needed from Product and Design, but nothing connected those needs to a plan for building them.
  • I had a second problem as the leader. With a team of ten, I could be blind to the reality of my team’s work on the ground.

Solution

Define what “good” looks like for each role, anchored in observable evidence, and assess it in the open with the team.

How it works

  • Six pillars. Learn, Envision, Plan, Execute, Measure and Influence cover the product lifecycle from understanding users and market through to influencing the organization. Each pillar breaks into scopes. Learn, for example, splits into voice-of-customer, market and product knowledge. [GAP: confirm the pillars were chosen to mirror the capabilities the business asked for.]
  • Anchored levels. Every scope has behavioural descriptions at four levels: Not Performing (0), Developing (3), Performing (6) and Outperforming (9). Shading marks in-between scores (+1 or −1), so a score always sits next to a description rather than a bare number.
  • Evidence built into the sheet. Each pillar lists the artifacts that count as proof (an interview register, a roadmap, an MBR/QBR). Notes and evidence columns sit beside the score so every score can be substantiated.
  • One rubric per role. I folded the framework into the competency ladders, so each role has its own rubric and expectations.
  • Scored together, in the open. I led the assessment with the team in our Community of Practice. The rubric was explicit about expectations, and the team was encouraged to argue for a higher score if they had evidence. The scoreboard was visible to departmental leadership.
  • An input, not a verdict. The scoreboard’s main job was to drive professional-excellence conversations and growth plans. It was one input to formal performance reviews, which also weighed external factors. High developer turnover on a team, for instance, affects a Product Manager’s ability to deliver on time, on budget and at quality.

Wins

  • Team members felt seen and cared for. I used the scoreboard as evidence in formal performance reviews.
  • It gave the team a venue to tell their own story of what they were accomplishing, which I couldn’t have seen from my seat.

Learning

  • Define “good” once, per role. An explicit, level-anchored standard makes growth discussable. Evidence, not impression, settles scores.
  • Make the assessment a venue, not a verdict. A leader can’t see all the work. Give people a place to tell their story with evidence, and let them contest a score.
  • Let the teacher be taught. Collaborative assessment teaches the assessor too. Engaging people in self- and peer-assessment makes them actors in their own improvement.
  • Score the person, then adjust for context. Weigh external factors separately, so the number stays honest and the review stays fair.

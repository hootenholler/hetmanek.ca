Opportunity

CX success belongs to everyone. But each department sees its own slice through its own lens, with its own budget and priorities

GS1 Canada serves six sectors, six subscriber types, and dozens of needs and tasks: complexity via 365 distinct user journeys. No one could say which journeys mattered most or how to efficiently and effectively improve them.

Meanwhile:

  • Resellers were out-funneling GS1 for people who just needed a barcode.
  • Billing issues generated an outsized percentage of annual support cases.
  • A $$$ attribute campaign led to only n subscribers adopting.
How do we get on the same page?

Solution

Stop mapping journeys one at a time.

Start understanding customers and users holistically via a matrix of touchpoints by journey, so the whole portfolio can be seen, filtered, and compared, then drilled into.

How it works

Conventional journey maps are hard to compare and contrast, especially at scale. A matrix lets you see the portfolio as a whole and also break any single journey into fine detail.

Structure:

  • Touchpoints (rows) are grouped by stage (i.e., Product Discovery, Sign Up, JTBD, Billing, off-boarding).
  • Each row carries: user needs, tasks, and expectations | value definition | links to VOC evidence (recordings, transcripts) | SWOT | Metrics and KPIs | maintenance costs | policy barriers | internal stakeholders | health score (derived from above) | a named caretaker
  • The matrix can be filtered by journeys, which are comprised of touch-points.

Two uses from one structure.

  1. Health. See journey and touchpoint health from a bird’s-eye view, including where patterns are consistent and where they aren’t.
  2. Discovery. That same analysis feeds research for designing solutions and for identifying and prioritizing the highest-value opportunities.

Wins

Learning

Bring a shared solution, not a mandate.
Large cross-functional work is political work, so design for that from the start. A neutral, customer-centered frame lets each department see its own part without giving up ownership.

Change the unit of analysis.
Comparison needs a common structure, so choose the structure before collecting the stories.

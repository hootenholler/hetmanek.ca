Opportunity Assessment Engine

Separate what you can do from what a stranger can verify. The difference is a work plan.

The tension

I was starting a new chapter. I wanted to make good things with cool people. I wanted engagement, fulfillment and money, in balance.

The search had four tensions:

  • Fit I couldn’t show. My experience and talent matched the roles I wanted, but a stranger reading a cold application probably couldn’t see it. I needed a way to see the gaps so I could decide which proof to build first.
  • Too many options. There are countless opportunities. How do I find fit, and how does fit find me?
  • Not every opportunity is a job. Personal projects compete for the same hours as professional ones (e.g., automating social media production for my wife’s podcast, or a small fashion business with my kids). Without a common measure, a personal project wins on enthusiasm and a job wins on urgency. Neither is a good reason.
  • Pressure and gut feel. Career decisions are made under pressure, especially when money tips the scales, and I’m susceptible. Conditions drive my behaviour more than grit. I don’t rise to the occasion so much as fall to the highest level of my preparation.

I needed a tool to provide structure and set those conditions, and I wanted it to be a piece of public proof in its own right.

The idea

Score opportunities on what I can do and want to do, and separately on what a stranger can verify. Rank the gaps across all of them, build the proof that closes the most important ones first, and apply myself where I’m both willing and winnable.

How it works

I am a product. So I started by building a positioning document that defines how my skills and profile meet market needs.

Then, in collaboration with Claude, I built skills, templates, and folders that run this assessment flow:

  1. Submit. Paste a posting or proposal into the engine.
  2. Gut feel. Record [Pursue], [Stretch] or [Decline] before seeing any score, so the score can’t anchor me.
  3. Gates: location, compensation floor, harm. Each returns Pass, Fail or Unknown, because an assumed pass is not a pass.
  4. Willingness. Do I want this? Three pillars (Compensation, Experience, Culture), with weights that shift as financial runway shortens.
  5. Winnability part 1:Fit. Can I do this? Each need in the posting maps to a row in my problem-capability matrix, valued by how strong the proof is.
  6. Winnability part 2: Visible fit. Can a stranger verify it? On a cold application, proof that isn’t public drops a level. A warm introduction restores it.
  7. Proof backlog. Across every assessment, the engine ranks which capability’s proof would unlock the most roles I want if it were public.
  8. Project Proposals use the same rubric. Fractional work, consulting and my own ideas arrive in one fixed shape: opportunity definition, outcomes, time and cost, positioning alignment. They pass the same gates and pillars as a job, so the two are comparable. A proposal can also be reshaped before I commit.
  9. Gaps named as gaps. When a need matches nothing in my matrix, I decide whether it’s a missing row or a real capability gap. For example, Leading Engineering at the executive level went down as a real gap.

What I decided against:

  • One score. A single number hides whether the problem is “I don’t want it”, “I can’t do it” or “I can’t prove it”. Each needs a different response.
  • A single winnability number. My first fix discounted private proof on cold applications. That made every cold posting score alike and hid real differences in match. Reporting Fit and Visible fit side by side kept both signals.
  • A separate rubric for personal projects. Two rubrics would mean two scales and no way to weigh a project against a job.
  • A fourth pillar for family and art. The podcast proposal mattered because of what it did for my family, which no pillar captured. I widened Culture instead, and will promote it to a pillar if it keeps coming up.
  • Waiting for win/loss data. With no live search, there were no outcomes to learn from. I calibrated on my own judgment first and built the outcomes log so data accumulates.
  • Blending my data into the framework. The rubric, skills and templates are the framework, designed to be forked on GitHub. My positioning, matrix, compensation floor and assessments are the instance, and stay private.

What Happened

  • The engine confirmed what I suspected, and sized it. On six cold job postings, Fit runs 4.25 to 4.53 out of 5 and Visible fit 2.75 to 3.07. On the one warm introduction, Visible fit (3.78) is higher than Fit (3.53).
  • Gut feel runs ahead of proof.. Where my gut and the engine differed, it was always the same way: I said Pursue, the engine said Stretch.
  • It produced a priority order. At first, 13 of my 14 capability rows had no public proof. The backlog ranks them by how often the roles I want ask for them.
  • I acted on it. After the first five assessments I paused applying and put my attention into building proof: this site, these case studies and personal projects. The engine didn’t change my mind. It validated the approach and told me where to start.
  • Fit started finding me. Recruiters began getting in touch, and I returned to assessing.
  • A personal project exposed a missing value. The podcast proposal scored in the band the rubric leaves to me, even though I clearly wanted to do it. The score was right and my stated values were incomplete, so I changed the definition of Culture.
  • The rubric changed three times in its first three days, each change logged with its reason.
  • What it can’t show yet. Every outcome is still open, so nothing here shows the scores predict results, and the framework isn’t on GitHub yet.

Learning

  • Conditions over grit. If a decision will be made under pressure, design it before the pressure arrives.
  • Measure what’s true and what’s visible apart. Capability nobody can verify doesn’t count with strangers. Score the two separately and the difference becomes a ranked to-do list.
  • A tool can earn its keep by confirming a hunch. Validation with a number and a priority order is what turns a belief into a plan.
  • What I’d do differently. I’d tie the project targets to the pace of the search instead of the calendar, and measure whether the engine told me what to work on next, not how many postings it scored. This project sits in a broader portfolio of life activity, and its priority changed. It stays open and will evolve as the search goes on.
